# Presentation speech (slide by slide)

## Slide 1: RAG Hallucination Detector
**Speaker: Kavay Khurana** · ~42 s

Good morning ma'am/sir. I am Kavay Khurana and this is Gurliv Singh. Our project is the RAG Hallucination Detector. Today, chatbots answer questions by first searching documents and then writing an answer from them. But sometimes they add facts that the documents never said. Our project uses machine learning to catch those invented sentences automatically. On the right you can see an example: the first sentence is supported, and the red one, 'It was designed by Leonardo da Vinci', is a hallucination that our model flags. Let me start with the problem.

## Slide 2: The problem: LLMs add facts the sources never said
**Speaker: Kavay Khurana** · ~48 s

RAG means Retrieval-Augmented Generation. A search step finds documents, called the context, and the LLM writes an answer using them. Even with the right context, the LLM can invent names, numbers or claims. That is a hallucination, and in a bank, hospital or college chatbot it can be dangerous. Checking every answer by hand is not possible. So our objectives were: first, build a labelled dataset in SQLite; second, design features that compare each sentence with the context and train classifiers; third, flag the risky sentences with a risk score in a web app; and fourth, test the detector on live answers from Google Gemini.

## Slide 3: Dataset: RAGTruth, 17,790 human-labelled answers
**Speaker: Kavay Khurana** · ~45 s

We used RAGTruth, a public benchmark published at ACL 2024 under the MIT licence. It has 17,790 responses written by 6 LLMs: GPT-4, GPT-3.5, three Llama-2 models and Mistral-7B, over 2,965 source contexts and three tasks: question answering, news summarisation and data-to-text writing. Humans marked the exact characters that are hallucinated. This chart, generated by our code from the SQLite database, shows that small open models hallucinate in about two thirds of answers, while GPT-4 and GPT-3.5 do so in about 14 percent. Data-to-text is the hardest task. Overall 43 percent of responses contain at least one hallucination.

## Slide 4: System architecture and pipeline
**Speaker: Kavay Khurana** · ~53 s

This is our pipeline. The backend downloads the two RAGTruth files and loads them into a SQLite database with four tables: sources, responses, runs and predictions, linked by a foreign key. Each response is split into sentences, and a sentence gets label 1 if it overlaps a human-marked span. We compute 10 features per sentence, train three classifiers, and save the best one with joblib. The Streamlit app loads that model, and every detection is logged back into SQLite. To avoid data leakage, we carved a dev set from train grouped by source passage, so all six answers to one passage stay together. That gives about 111 thousand training sentences, 11 thousand dev and 20 thousand test.

## Slide 5: Features: how different is a sentence from its context?
**Speaker: Kavay Khurana** · ~41 s

The heart of the project is feature engineering. For every sentence we ask: how much of it comes from the context? Novelty features count content words, numbers and capitalised names that never appear in the context. Overlap features measure how many 2-word and 3-word phrases are copied from the context. Position features capture that LLMs often add extra claims near the end. The chart proves the features carry signal: hallucinated sentences have a novel-word ratio of 0.51 versus 0.30 for grounded ones, and their trigram overlap is only 0.06 versus 0.21.

## Slide 6: Models: three classifiers, Gradient Boosting selected
**Speaker: Kavay Khurana** · ~49 s

We compared three supervised classifiers. Logistic Regression is our linear baseline. Random Forest averages 300 independent trees. Gradient Boosting, using scikit-learn's HistGradientBoostingClassifier, builds trees one after another, each fixing the errors of the previous ones. Only 11 percent of sentences are hallucinated, so we used balanced class weights. For each model we tuned the decision threshold on the dev set for the best F1, and picked the model with the highest dev F1, which was Gradient Boosting. Early stopping ended its training at 148 trees, and its threshold is 0.66. The test set was only used at the very end for reporting. Now Gurliv will present the results.

## Slide 7: Results: ROC-AUC 0.80 on the held-out test set
**Speaker: Gurliv Singh** · ~45 s

Thank you Kavay. Here are the results on the held-out test set, at response level, which answers the practical question: should I trust this answer? Gradient Boosting has the best F1 of 0.664 and ROC-AUC of 0.80. AUC 0.80 means that if we take one hallucinated and one clean answer, the model ranks the hallucinated one higher 80 percent of the time. It catches 79 percent of hallucinated answers. By task, data-to-text reaches F1 0.78, question answering 0.55, and summaries 0.38, because summaries paraphrase a lot. Sentence-level F1 is lower, 0.34, since only 8 percent of test sentences are hallucinated.

## Slide 8: LLM management: Gemini writes, our model judges
**Speaker: Gurliv Singh** · ~57 s

LLMs play two roles in our project. The six LLMs in RAGTruth only produced the training data. Live, we use Google Gemini as the answer writer, and our trained model is the judge. The user types a question. If no passages are given, Gemini first writes them, like a search engine. Then Gemini answers, either strictly from the passages, or with outside facts added to provoke hallucinations, and our model checks every sentence. We call Gemini's REST API directly. The API key stays in a git-ignored secrets file and is sent in a header. Busy or rate-limited calls are retried with back-off, then the app switches to the other Gemini model. A bad key fails fast, and in tests Gemini is faked, so they run offline.

## Slide 9: Live demo: the Streamlit app
**Speaker: Gurliv Singh** · ~54 s

This is our Streamlit app. Top left, the Invented fact demo: the context says the Eiffel Tower is in Paris, completed in 1889, and the answer adds that it was designed by Leonardo da Vinci. The model shades that sentence red with probability 0.87 and underlines the words not found in the context. Top right, the Wrong numbers demo: 1925 and 500 metres are flagged. At the bottom is a real RAGTruth test answer: the red sentence is our model's prediction and the yellow part is the human annotation, and they match. The app also has Dataset, Model Performance and History tabs, read live from SQLite, and all 7 UI tests pass. We'll show it live in a moment.

## Slide 10: Real-life use cases
**Speaker: Gurliv Singh** · ~42 s

Where can this be used? Any place an LLM answers from documents. A customer-support bot answering from a refund policy. A healthcare assistant answering from a drug leaflet, where an invented dosage is dangerous. Legal teams summarising contracts. Newsrooms checking auto-generated summaries for invented quotes and numbers. A college help desk answering from the academic handbook. And companies can log the risk score of every answer, like our History tab, to monitor and compare LLMs over time. The detector is fast and runs locally, so it can sit in front of any chatbot.

## Slide 12: Conclusion, future work and references
**Speaker: Gurliv Singh** · ~41 s

To conclude: we built a complete, tested pipeline from raw data to a working web app, and our Gradient Boosting model detects hallucinated answers with ROC-AUC 0.80 using only 10 simple, explainable features. Its limitation is that word-matching cannot see a contradiction that reuses context words, for example swapping two numbers. Our next step would be a transformer such as DeBERTa, or an NLI entailment score as an extra feature. These are our main references. Thank you for listening. We are happy to answer your questions and show the live demo.
